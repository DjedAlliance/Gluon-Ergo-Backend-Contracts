## Findings

### Finding 1: Beta decays can push the reactor out of the healthy range, letting holders force permissionless peg adjustments — `modules/gluonw-base/src/main/resources/ErgoContracts/GluonW/Ergs/BoxGuardScripts/GluonWBoxGuardScript.es:121`

- Severity: Medium
- Confidence: 0.8
- Category: economic_manipulation / oracle_and_state_validation
- Description: The healthy-range gate `isHealthy` (line 121) is computed only from the **input** box. `q` uses `supplyNeutrons` from `GLUON_BOX.tokens(1)` and `reserve` from `GLUON_BOX.value` (lines 110–119). Both beta-decay branches require `isHealthy` (lines 434–444 and 545–555), but neither checks the fusion ratio of the **output** box. A single beta decay that starts inside `[0.50, 0.98]` can therefore leave the reactor outside that range. The Adjust Peg branch (lines 557–568) needs no signature and only requires the *input* state to be unhealthy. It has no cooldown, no per-epoch limit and no record of when the peg last changed. Each 1% step is a separate transaction, and the steps can be chained in the same block until `q` is back inside the range.

  Lowering the peg factor lowers the target price of every neutron (`priceAdjusted`, line 118, which drives `qNorm`, `valueOfNeutrons` and the beta-decay exchange rates). The reserve is unchanged, so that value moves to proton holders (proton price `(1 − qNorm)·R/Sp`). Raising the peg factor does the opposite. The volume-dependent beta-decay fee (`VarPhiBeta`, lines 405 and 516) scales with the size of the trade that pushes `q` across the threshold. It does not scale with the size of the peg move that trade unlocks. Near a threshold, a small and cheap beta decay therefore triggers a peg change affecting the entire neutron supply.

  The header (line 43) documents that any user may adjust the peg *when* `q` is outside the range. Nothing documents that trades may push `q` outside the range themselves. The comment on line 121 states that beta decays are "only permitted when 0.50 <= q <= 0.98". This is therefore treated as a finding, not a design choice.
- Exploit Scenario: The reactor is at `q ≈ 0.975`, close to the upper threshold, for example after a fall in the ERG price. At this `q`, the whole proton supply is worth only about 2.5% of the reserve, so the attacker can cheaply accumulate about half of it. In one block the attacker:
  1. Submits a BetaDecay+ that converts protons worth about 0.56% of the reserve into neutrons. It is accepted because the *pre-trade* `q` is 0.975; the post-trade `q` is about 0.9805.
  2. Chains an Adjust Peg transaction. The input `q` is above 0.98, so the peg factor must drop by 1%. `q` becomes about 0.971 and the reactor is healthy again.
  3. Optionally chains a BetaDecay− to convert the freshly minted neutrons back into protons. This also offsets the volume register, keeping the next round's fee low.

  A real-number model of the contract's formulas gives these figures for one round. The attacker pays about 0.01% of the reserve in fees (the beta-decay fee of about 1.06% plus the oracle fee and the maximum dev fee). Neutron holders lose about 0.97% of the reserve, a permanent 1% cut to the peg. The attacker's position gains about 27%, roughly 0.33% of the reserve. A second round is still profitable, with diminishing returns as volume fees build up and the attacker's protons are used up. The 14-day volume window then resets.

  The mirror attack works at the lower threshold. A neutron holder uses a BetaDecay− to push `q` just below 0.50, then forces +1% peg steps. The model shows smaller gains there, about 0.1–0.2% of the reserve per round for a holder of 30% of the neutrons.
- Recommendation:
  1. Check health on the **post-transaction** state for beta decays, and reject trades that end outside the healthy range. This also stops a single beta decay from pushing `q` beyond `qStar`. For example:
     ```scala
     val outSupplyNeutrons: BigInt = (NEUTRONS_TOTAL_SUPPLY - OUT_NEUTRONS_TOKEN._2).toBigInt
     val outReserve: BigInt        = (OUT_GLUON_BOX.value - _MinFee).toBigInt
     val qOut: BigInt              = outSupplyNeutrons * priceAdjusted / outReserve
     val isHealthyAfter: Boolean   = (qOut >= qLowerThreshold) && (qOut <= qUpperThreshold)
     ```
     Add `isHealthyAfter` to the BetaDecay+ and BetaDecay− conjunctions, and consider adding it to fission and fusion for consistency.
  2. Rate-limit Adjust Peg so that peg moves follow the oracle rather than trades. For example, store the height of the last peg adjustment in a register field and require a minimum number of blocks between adjustments. Alternatively, allow at most one adjustment per oracle update, e.g. by requiring `ORACLE_BOX.creationInfo._1` to be greater than the stored height of the last adjustment.

### Finding 2: Oracle data input is not authenticated in fission and fusion, so the healthy-range gate can be bypassed — `modules/gluonw-base/src/main/resources/ErgoContracts/GluonW/Ergs/BoxGuardScripts/GluonWBoxGuardScript.es:276`

- Severity: Low
- Confidence: 0.9
- Category: oracle_validation
- Description: `price` (line 115) is read from `CONTEXT.dataInputs(0).R4[Long]`, and `isHealthy` (line 121) depends on it. The check that data input 0 really is the oracle pool box is `cOracle` (lines 183–186), which verifies the pool NFT and freshness. `cOracle` is part of the BetaDecay+ (line 443), BetaDecay− (line 554) and Adjust Peg (line 565) conjunctions. It is **absent** from the fission conjunction (lines 276–282) and the fusion conjunction (lines 304–310), even though both require `isHealthy`. In fission and fusion, the spender can therefore supply any box with a `Long` in R4 as data input 0, and so choose the price that `isHealthy` is evaluated against. If the compiler hoists `cOracle` and evaluates it eagerly, the box may also need at least one token so that `ORACLE_BOX.tokens(0)` does not fail; that is trivial to arrange. The healthy-range circuit breaker that the contract states for fission and fusion (comment on line 121) is therefore unenforceable for those two operations. No direct theft path was identified: fission and fusion token amounts (lines 270–271 and 294–299) and their fees do not depend on the price. Hence the Low severity.
- Exploit Scenario: According to the real oracle, the reactor is unhealthy, say `q = 1.05` after an ERG crash, and the protocol intends every operation except Adjust Peg to be frozen. The attacker creates a box holding any one token, with R4 set to a price that makes the computed `q` equal 0.9. They use that box as data input 0 in a fission or fusion transaction, and the reactor accepts the operation the protocol intended to block.
- Recommendation: Add `cOracle` to the fission and fusion conjunctions (lines 276–282 and 304–310). If fission and fusion are meant to be independent of the oracle, remove `isHealthy` from those two branches instead, and update the comment on line 121 so the intended behaviour is explicit.

## Notes

### Note 1: UI-fee recipient and payment are chosen by the spender — `modules/gluonw-base/src/main/resources/ErgoContracts/GluonW/Ergs/BoxGuardScripts/GluonWBoxGuardScript.es:242`

- Category: design-tradeoff
- Observation: `_optUIFeeAddress = getVar[SigmaProp](0)` is a context-extension variable supplied by whoever builds the transaction. The UI-fee output (lines 243–253) is checked only when the variable is present. Any user who builds a transaction directly can omit the UI fee, or direct it to themselves.
- Rationale: The UI fee is explicitly optional. The header lists `_optUIFeeAddress` as an optional context variable (lines 35–36), the parameter comment reads "Optional UI fee: 0.4%" (line 107), and the off-chain `GluonWFees.getFeesOutBox` adds a UI-fee box only when a UI address is given. The user pays the fee on top of the trade; it never comes out of the reserve, so omitting it does not affect protocol funds.

### Note 2: Beta decays and peg adjustments accept an oracle price up to 34 blocks old — `modules/gluonw-base/src/main/resources/ErgoContracts/GluonW/Ergs/BoxGuardScripts/GluonWBoxGuardScript.es:184`

- Category: accepted-risk
- Observation: `cOracle` accepts the oracle pool box if it was created within the last 34 blocks (about 68 minutes). If the market price moves by more than the beta-decay fees within that window, before the pool box is refreshed, a trader can beta-decay against the reserve at the stale price.
- Rationale: The freshness window is an explicit, documented parameter (comment on line 184). Data inputs must be unspent boxes, so only the current pool box can be used. The minimum fees bound the size of profitable stale-price trades: 0.5% `phi0`, plus the 0.1% oracle fee, plus up to 0.5% dev fee. This is the standard latency trade-off for oracle-priced stablecoin designs.

## Limitations

**Standing limitations**

- This review does not replace a full manual audit or a penetration test.
- This is static analysis of source text. Nothing was executed, fuzzed, or traced at runtime, so timing-dependent, load-dependent, or infrastructure-dependent issues cannot be confirmed here.
- Confidence scores are the reviewing model's own self-assessment, not a calibrated statistical probability.
- Absence of a reported finding does not mean the code is secure. Safety guardrails built into some models can cause a real vulnerability to be softened, generalized, or left out rather than reported in exploit-level detail, and training-data bias can make some vulnerability classes easier for a model to recognize than others. Treat this report as a floor on what a full audit would find, not a ceiling.
- Findings reflect the reviewing model's training data cutoff. A vulnerability tied to a very recently disclosed CVE or library advisory may not be recognized as such.

**Review-specific limitations**

- **Scope.** Only `GluonWBoxGuardScript.es` was reviewed. The off-chain Scala code and `GluonWTxProxyContract.es` were consulted for context only and were not reviewed.
- **Oracle buyback and oracle pool contracts unavailable.** Neither contract's source is in the repository; their addresses come from deployment configuration. The oracle-fee check (lines 212–223) identifies the buyback input only by position (the last input) and by script, and checks the buyback NFT only on the output. Whether this can be abused, for example with a decoy box carrying the buyback script as the last input, depends on the buyback contract's own rules, which could not be evaluated. That the pool box's R4 holds a price in nanoERG per kg was also not verified.
- **Deployment-time constants not verified.** `_MinFee`, `_OracleFeePk`, `_OraclePoolNFT` and `_OracleBuybackNFT` are substituted at deployment, and their values were not checked. For example, `_OracleFeePk` is compared with `propositionBytes` but is substituted from the payment address's `contentBytes` (`GluonWBoxContract.scala`, line 26). Those bytes equal the full ErgoTree only for a P2S address.
- **No compiler or runtime.** No ErgoScript compiler was available (sbt is not installed), so the contract was neither compiled nor executed. Statements about evaluation semantics are based on the reviewing model's understanding of `sigma-state` 5.0.8, not on runtime verification. This covers eager evaluation of hoisted top-level `val`s, failure on missing registers or tokens, and exact (overflow-checked) `Long` arithmetic.
- **Economic figures are modelled.** The figures in Finding 1 come from a simplified real-number model of the contract's formulas written for this review. It ignores integer rounding, miner fees and market reactions, and it assumes a fresh volume window and the maximum dev fee. The figures are illustrative, not an on-chain simulation.
- No exploit-level detail was withheld from any finding.
